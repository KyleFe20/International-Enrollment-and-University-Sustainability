# Path A 

## Archetype: Growth and Underinvestment 

The system archetype of growth and underinvestment helps explain why student housing in Halifax continues to experience increasing pressure over time. Each year, more students are drawn to the city due to its universities and overall appeal, steadily increasing the demand for housing. However, the supply of student housing, along with institutional and government support, has not expanded at the same pace. This creates a structural imbalance between demand and supply.
As demand rises without a proportional increase in available housing, vacancy rates decline, making it more difficult for students to secure accommodation. At the same time, rental prices increase, further limiting accessibility, particularly for students with tighter budgets. This forms a reinforcing loop: higher enrollment leads to greater housing demand, which reduces vacancy rates and intensifies housing pressure.

A key issue within this archetype is the delay in response. Even when policymakers and institutions recognize the growing demand, investments in housing infrastructure require significant time to plan and implement. By the time new housing becomes available, demand has often already surpassed it.  A balancing loop emerges, where rising housing pressure triggers increased investment and support, improving availability and reducing strain. However, due to these delays the balancing response is consistently too slow to be effective.

As a result, the system becomes reactive rather than proactive. Instead of stabilizing the housing market grows increasingly strained, damaging its sustainability in the long run. Without early and proactive investment in housing development and support services, the gap between student demand and available housing will increase, making the problem more complex and expensive to solve in the long run.

Reinforcing loop: More enrollment leads to higher housing demand, which lowers vacancy rates and increases housing pressure.

Balancing loop: Higher housing pressure should trigger more housing support and supply, which would increase availability and reduce pressure.

## Status Quo: 

If the Nova Scotia Minister of Advanced Education does not make a significant policy change, Halifax will likely remain the most stressed student housing market in the province over the next five to ten years. This outcome reflects the reinforcing loop, where increasing student enrollment drives higher housing demand, which in turn lowers vacancy rates and intensifies competition for available units. Based on current trends shown in the line chart, vacancy rates in Halifax could remain below 2 to 3 percent, which is widely considered a very tight housing market. As more students compete for limited housing, rental prices are likely to continue rising, making affordability an increasing concern.

There is also a clear delay within the system. Even if new housing developments are approved, construction takes time, meaning supply cannot respond immediately to the rising demand. While smaller university centers such as Antigonish and Cape Breton may also face localized housing shortages, the concentration of students in Halifax will keep overall pressure highest in that market.

A key uncertainty in this scenario is whether enrollment growth will stabilize or continue to increase over the next five to ten years. If enrollment continues to rise without a corresponding increase in housing supply, conditions are likely to worsen. One potential unintended consequence is increased overcrowding, as students may be forced to share spaces or accept lower quality housing. Over time this could reduce student satisfaction and negatively impact the reputation of universities in Nova Scotia, particularly for prospective students considering studying in Halifax

## Intervention A: Prioritize Halifax 

If the Minister chooses to prioritize Halifax, the policy would directly target the region experiencing the highest level of student housing pressure. In the beginning, the impact would be limited due to delays in the housing development process. Planning, approvals, and construction all take time, so vacancy rates may remain below 3 percent initially, and students may not feel immediate relief.

As new housing supply enters the market, the system dynamics start to shift. The strong reinforcing loop, where rising enrollment increases housing pressure begins to weaken. During this time the balancing loop is introduced, as increased housing supply improves vacancy rates and reduces competition among students. Over time, vacancy rates could rise to around 4 to 5 percent which would significantly ease pressure in the Halifax housing market.

In five to ten years the system will be more stable. The balancing loop would begin to dominate, as housing supply better aligns with student demand. This would reduce overall housing stress and improve affordability and availability for students in Halifax.However, there are still important uncertainties. One key question is whether student enrollment will continue to grow faster than expected. If demand continues to rise rapidly, even increased housing supply may struggle to keep up. Additionally, an unintended consequence of focusing heavily on Halifax is that smaller university centers, such as Antigonish and Cape Breton, may receive less attention and investment. This could cause housing pressures to shift to those regions over time, creating new challenges elsewhere in the province.

## Intervention B: Multi Center Support 

If the Minister distributes housing support across multiple centers, the system is likely to develop more evenly across the province. In the beginning, there would be limited change due to delays in implementation, such as planning, approvals, and construction timelines. As a result, vacancy rates would likely remain low across Halifax, Antigonish, and Cape Breton in the early stages.

As the operation continues, smaller increases in housing supply would begin to appear in each center. This would create several balancing loops, where increases in supply help reduce housing pressure within each center. However, because investment is spread across multiple locations, the impact in Halifax where demand is highest, may be less significant. Vacancy rates in Halifax could remain below 3 percent, meaning housing pressure would still be relatively high compared to other centers.

Over a five to ten year period, housing conditions would gradually improve across all centers, leading to a more balanced system. However, no single region would experience a major shift or rapid improvement. Overall pressure may remain elevated, particularly in Halifax, due to its consistently high demand. A key uncertainty in this scenario is whether student demand continues to concentrate in Halifax despite increased support in other regions. If so, the policy may not fully address the core issue. One unintended consequence is that spreading resources too thin could reduce the overall effectiveness of the intervention, especially in the area with the greatest need, which would limit the potential for meaningful impact.

## Leverage Point Analysis:

The strongest leverage point in this system is the targeted expansion of student housing in Halifax. This is the most effective point of intervention because Halifax has both the highest concentration of students and the greatest housing pressure. For the Nova Scotia Minister of Advanced Education, this means that prioritizing Halifax is the most strategic use of limited resources, as it directly addresses where demand is most intense and where policy action will have the greatest overall impact.

This leverage point influences both key system dynamics. First, it reduces the reinforcing pressure loop by increasing the supply of available housing, which helps ease competition and improve vacancy rates. Next, it strengthens the balancing loop, by aligning housing development closely with rising demand. Rentals also provide immediate relief to students while housing developments are being completed. This allows the Minister to address both short-term pressures and long-term system stability at the same time.

However, there are several risks the Minister must consider when making this decision. One major challenge is the time delay involved in housing development. Even with strong policy support, planning, approvals, and construction can take several years, meaning the benefits will not be immediate. There are also practical constraints, such as limited land availability and rising construction costs, which may slow progress. Also, prioritizing Halifax could raise political concerns because other regions may feel that they are being overlooked. Despite these risks, targeting Halifax remains the most impactful policy choice for the Minister, because it focuses on the core driver of the problem rather than distributing resources too broadly, which could reduce the overall effectiveness of the intervention.
